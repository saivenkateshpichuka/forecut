Methodology

How we forecast.

What Forecut's labels mean, where the evidence comes from, and how often each forecast is checked and revised.

Three kinds of signal

  • Observed: what is in stores and on runways now (the current season, and Resort collections already shown). Based on published runway, retail and trade reporting.
  • Forecast: the next season, based on published runway and trade research for that season, read by Forecut for timing and adoption.
  • Projection: long-range seasons (one to two years ahead). Forecut's own projection of where current signals lead; updated as runway coverage arrives.

Trend stages

Every detail carries one stage per season, and the same four names are used on every page:

  • Emerging: early signals in a few collections or markets. Watch and test in small quantities.
  • Rising: spreading across designers, retailers and markets. Add it now.
  • Peaking: at full adoption. Use it in core styles; expect it to cool.
  • Fading: past its peak. Phase it out of new development. Fading details stay visible so you can see the full lifecycle.

How stages are assigned and revised

We look for the same detail across several independent sources: runway reviews, trade and retail coverage, brand launches, fashion week schedules and search interest. A detail moves up a stage when it appears in more collections, more markets or more retail ranges; it moves down when coverage and new launches slow. Stages are reviewed monthly. News is checked daily and new details are added weekly. We never delete a detail: past stages are kept so you can follow its whole curve.

Regional readings

A global trend is not equally relevant everywhere. Each region page reads the international seasons for its own climate, calendar and buyers: southern-hemisphere markets sell a season roughly six months later; hot-climate markets get lighter versions and skip heavy winter pieces; and buying peaks around local moments such as Diwali, Ramadan, Lunar New Year, Black Friday or carnival.

Sources

Forecut uses public sources only: fashion week calendars and runway reviews, trade press, retailer and brand announcements, company results, and colour authorities. Source links are given on region pages, reports and articles. We do not copy paid forecasting services, and we write every forecast in our own words. Brands and designers are named only where public sources report them.

Images

Most images on Forecut are AI-generated illustrations made for Forecut to show a detail clearly. They are not runway, campaign or brand photographs. Real photographs are used only when they are free-licensed (for example from Unsplash, Pexels, Wikimedia Commons or The Met Open Access) and are credited where required.

Dates

Season pages show two dates: last researched (when the evidence behind the forecast was last reviewed) and data refreshed (when the page was last rebuilt).

Limits

Forecasts are informed opinions for planning, not guarantees. Check them against your own sales data, costings and customers. Price guides are indicative estimates.

A worked example

Take a single detail such as a halter neckline. In one week it might appear in a São Paulo show, a Delhi gown and a US retailer's new range. One sighting is a signal; three independent sightings across different regions and price levels is evidence. We record each source, check whether the detail is new or a repeat of last season, and only then set a stage. If it shows up mainly on runways, it starts as emerging. Once high-street and online retailers carry it in several markets, it moves to rising. When it is in most ranges and discounting begins, it is peaking.

How to read a lifecycle

Every detail has one letter per season, read from left to right. A path such as emerging, rising, peaking tells you to build stock gradually. A detail that skips a season (shown as a gap) is seasonal: a summer fabric, for example, that returns each spring. A detail marked fading is not wrong to sell, but new development money is usually better spent elsewhere.

Regional weighting

We do not average the world. A trend seen only in Europe stays emerging globally even if it is peaking in Milan, and region pages note where adoption is ahead or behind. Climate also changes the reading: heavy pieces are scaled down for tropical markets, and southern-hemisphere stores are given a timing shift rather than a separate forecast.

Common questions

Do you use sales data? Not from retailers' private systems. We use public results, launches and retail coverage. Combine our stages with your own sell-through data.

Why are some brands named and others not? We only name a brand when a public source reports that it showed or sold the detail.

What happens when a forecast is wrong? We revise the stage at the next monthly review and keep the earlier stage on record.
