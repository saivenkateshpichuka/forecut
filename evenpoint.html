How to decide an order quantity

A factory minimum order is a commitment, not a sales forecast. Enter the minimum order or the number of pieces you plan to make, the variable production cost per piece, the fixed costs that come with the collection (samples, photography, patterns, launch marketing) and your expected sell-through at full price and on sale. The calculator shows the upfront investment, the profit at your planned sell-through and the sell-through you need just to break even. If break-even sits above about 70% for a new style, the order is risky: most small labels sell a meaningful share on discount or not at all.

You can also add a recovery value for unsold stock, such as outlet or bulk sale, as a percentage of net revenue per piece. Keep it modest; leftover seasonal stock rarely recovers more than its cost.

How the maths changes by market

  • India: marketplace commission, cash-on-delivery and high return rates on fashion mean net proceeds per piece can be far below the ticket price, so take figures from the Price & Profit Calculator first.
  • Europe: strict returns rules and free-returns expectations raise the return burden; test a lower first order and reorder winners.
  • North America: heavy promotional periods such as Black Friday push more units into discount, so model a planned discount as well as full price.
  • Asia sourcing hubs: Chinese, Bangladeshi and Vietnamese factories often offer lower unit costs at higher minimums; compare two quantities side by side rather than taking the cheapest unit price.

Common questions

Why does a bigger order look less profitable? More pieces lower the unit cost but raise the investment and the share that has to sell. If sell-through falls, profit can drop even though each piece is cheaper.

Does it include tax? Revenue is shown excluding product tax so margins are comparable across markets; enter prices accordingly.
